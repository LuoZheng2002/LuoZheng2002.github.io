<section class="section" id="research" aria-labelledby="research-heading"><h2 class="section-heading" id="research-heading">Research Interest</h2><div class="section-intro"><p>My long-term research interest can be summarized as general intelligence like LLMs that are more efficient, more reliable, more predictable, and more "logically beautiful" (see more <a href="detailed_research_interest/">here</a>). I envision my ultimate goal to not be adding incremental improvements on existing systems (like transformer architecture) regarding efficiency, etc., but to found a fundamentally new paradigm that eliminates the structural limitations of the traditional connectionist (deep learning) paradigm, like being data-hungry and lacking safety guarantee, by making bold sacrifices (like devoting much more manual effort in addition to automatic learning). However, this is very hard and potentially requires long-term trials and errors. Therefore, to make things realistic, I am open to any theoretical (as opposed to applied) AI fields as research directions for my Ph.D studies, especially LLM training and fine-tuning, and multimodal generative models including image, video and 3D model generation, and seek to learn the frontier approaches, be up-to-date with latest ideas, and gradually migrate useful insights to my own research direction. <a href="ai_field_understanding/">See my preliminary understanding of the current general-purpose AI field</a> and <a href="long_term_research_plan/">my long-term research plan</a> for more.</p><p>My short-term research plan for my Ph.D studies can be found <a href="short_term_research_plan/">here</a>.</p></div></section>

<section class="section" id="publications" aria-labelledby="publications-heading"><h2 class="section-heading" id="publications-heading">Publications and Research Experiences</h2><div class="paper-list">
<article class="paper"><img class="paper-thumb" src="papers/lost_in_execution_image.png" alt="Thumbnail image for lost in execution"><div><h3 class="paper-title">Lost in Execution: On the Multilingual Robustness of Tool Calling in Large Language Models</h3><span class="paper-status">Published - ACL 2026</span><span class="paper-author-tag">First Author</span><p class="paper-meta"><a href="papers/lost_in_execution.pdf" download>Download paper</a> · <a href="https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.2039/">Venue page</a></p><details><summary>Project description</summary><div class="paper-detail-content"><img class="detail-image" src="papers/lost_in_execution_image.png" alt="Detail image for lost in execution"><div class="paper-description"><p><strong>Lost in Execution</strong> studies <strong>multilingual robustness</strong> in LLM tool calling. The paper introduces <strong>MLCL</strong>, a diagnostic benchmark across English, Chinese, Hindi, and Igbo, and shows that models may understand intent and select the right tool but still fail because parameter values are not executable. The key failure mode is <strong>parameter-value language mismatch</strong>, where semantically correct non-English arguments violate tool-interface conventions.</p><p>I am the <strong>first author</strong> of this <strong>ACL 2026</strong> paper. I built the benchmark, designed the <strong>fine-grained error taxonomy</strong>, analyzed failures across intent, tool selection, parameter extraction, and execution mismatch, and evaluated mitigation strategies. This project highlighted my ability to connect benchmark design with practical agent reliability.</p></div></div></details></div></article>
<article class="paper"><img class="paper-thumb" src="papers/treempo_image.png" alt="Thumbnail image for treempo"><div><h3 class="paper-title">TreeMPO: Fine-Grained Credit Assignment for LLM Training via Token-Level Trajectory Branching</h3><span class="paper-status">Manuscript</span><span class="paper-author-tag">First Author</span><p class="paper-meta"><a href="papers/treempo.pdf" download>Download paper</a></p><details><summary>Project description</summary><div class="paper-detail-content"><img class="detail-image" src="papers/treempo_image.png" alt="Detail image for treempo"><div class="paper-description"><p><strong>TreeMPO</strong> studies <strong>fine-grained credit assignment</strong> for long LLM reasoning trajectories. Instead of assigning one reward to an entire response, it branches token-level trajectories, estimates <strong>segment-level contribution</strong> from mixed-success descendants, and converts the evidence into token-level advantages for LoRA/RL fine-tuning. The method supports mathematical reasoning and <strong>tool-calling traces</strong>, with consistent validation gains across several model families.</p><p>I am the <strong>first author</strong> of this manuscript in preparation. I designed the core TreeMPO algorithm, including the <strong>token-level branching strategy</strong>, contribution model, and advantage-generation pipeline, and built the full system for rollout collection, trajectory-tree construction, LoRA fine-tuning, and checkpoint validation under constrained GPU resources.</p></div></div></details></div></article>
<article class="paper"><img class="paper-thumb" src="papers/street_level_image.png" alt="Thumbnail image for street level"><div><h3 class="paper-title">Street-Level Competitive Ride-Hailing with Fixed-Rival Defensive PSRO</h3><span class="paper-status">Submitted - AAAI 2027</span><span class="paper-author-tag">First Author</span><p class="paper-meta"><a href="papers/street_level.pdf" download>Download paper</a></p><details><summary>Project description</summary><div class="paper-detail-content"><img class="detail-image" src="papers/street_level_image.png" alt="Detail image for street level"><div class="paper-description"><p><strong>Street-Level Competitive Ride-Hailing with Fixed-Rival Defensive PSRO</strong> studies competitive fleet management at <strong>street-level resolution</strong> rather than coarse zone level. The paper introduces a <strong>SUMO-backed competitive ride-hailing simulator</strong> calibrated with NYC TLC and OpenStreetMap data, evaluates structured and residual policy designs, and proposes <strong>Fixed-Rival Defensive PSRO</strong> for robustness against strategically difficult rivals.</p><p>I am the <strong>first author</strong> of this <strong>AAAI 2027 submission</strong>. I developed the simulation framework, designed street-level controller features for relocation, pricing, and service decisions, implemented Manhattan-scale evaluation pipelines, and proposed the <strong>FRDF-PSRO</strong> protocol for robust multi-agent decision-making under competitive pressure.</p></div></div></details></div></article>
<article class="paper"><img class="paper-thumb" src="papers/fairness_or_fluency_image.png" alt="Thumbnail image for fairness or fluency"><div><h3 class="paper-title">Fairness or Fluency? An Investigation into Language Bias of Pairwise LLM-as-a-Judge</h3><span class="paper-status">Submitted - NeurIPS 2026 Workshop JUDGe</span><p class="paper-meta"><a href="papers/fairness_or_fluency.pdf" download>Download paper</a></p><details><summary>Project description</summary><div class="paper-detail-content"><img class="detail-image" src="papers/fairness_or_fluency_image.png" alt="Detail image for fairness or fluency"><div class="paper-description"><p><strong>Fairness or Fluency?</strong> studies <strong>language bias</strong> in pairwise LLM-as-a-judge evaluation. The paper finds performance disparities across language families and shows that many models prefer English answers in cross-language comparisons. It further tests whether this bias is explained by answer perplexity, finding that perplexity is correlated with bias but <strong>does not fully explain language preference</strong>.</p><p>I contributed to this <strong>NeurIPS 2026 Workshop JUDGe submission</strong> as a co-author. My role focused on designing and implementing experiments that relate judge preference to answer perplexity, using <strong>regression-based analysis</strong> to separate language identity from fluency effects, and improving perplexity collection for more reliable multilingual evaluation.</p></div></div></details></div></article>
<article class="paper"><img class="paper-thumb" src="papers/sim_2_real_image.png" alt="Thumbnail image for sim 2 real"><div><h3 class="paper-title">When Simulation Lies: A Sim-to-Real Benchmark and Domain-Randomized RL Recipe for Tool-Use Agents</h3><span class="paper-status">Published - NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations and Datasets Track, poster</span><p class="paper-meta"><a href="papers/sim_2_real.pdf" download>Download paper</a> · <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11928">arXiv page</a></p><details><summary>Project description</summary><div class="paper-detail-content"><img class="detail-image" src="papers/sim_2_real_image.png" alt="Detail image for sim 2 real"><div class="paper-description"><p><strong>When Simulation Lies / RobustBench-TC</strong> frames tool-use robustness as a <strong>sim-to-real problem</strong>. It introduces a production-grounded benchmark with perturbations organized by POMDP components: observation, action space, reward-relevant metadata, and transition dynamics. Across 21 models, the study finds that reward-relevant and transition perturbations cause much larger failures than simple observation noise, and proposes <strong>ToolRL-DR</strong> to narrow robustness gaps through perturbation-augmented RL.</p><p>I contributed to this <strong>NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations and Datasets Track poster</strong> as a co-author. My work included implementing user-query perturbations on ACEBench, supporting robustness experiments under deployment-like noise, and contributing to the analysis showing that <strong>perturbation-augmented RL</strong> can improve tool-use robustness beyond clean benchmark accuracy.</p></div></div></details></div></article>
<article class="paper"><img class="paper-thumb" src="papers/latent_skill_image.png" alt="Thumbnail image for latent skill"><div><h3 class="paper-title">SkiLT: What Agent Reinforcement Learning Actually Learns from a Latent Skill Library</h3><span class="paper-status">Submitted - AAAI 2027</span><p class="paper-meta"><a href="papers/latent_skill.pdf" download>Download paper</a></p><details><summary>Project description</summary><div class="paper-detail-content"><img class="detail-image" src="papers/latent_skill_image.png" alt="Detail image for latent skill"><div class="paper-description"><p><strong>SkiLT</strong> studies whether retrieved text-skill libraries for LLM agents can be compressed into <strong>compact latent skill tokens</strong> during reinforcement learning. It reduces the skill section from roughly <strong>970 tokens to 140 tokens per step</strong> while maintaining performance comparable to text-skill RL checkpoints on ALFWorld. The mechanism analysis further shows that the actor may adapt around nearly fixed skill rows, complicating the interpretation of what latent skill conditioning actually contributes.</p><p>I contributed to this <strong>AAAI 2027 submission</strong> as a co-author. My work included designing and implementing <strong>latent-form skill compression</strong>, integrating compact skill tokens into the GRPO workflow, evaluating against text-skill RL baselines, and helping analyze training dynamics to understand what agent RL actually learns from auxiliary skill memory.</p></div></div></details></div></article>
